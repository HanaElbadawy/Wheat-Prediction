Built for decisions, not demos

Three commitments behind every number on this page.

01 — relatedness

Additive and dominance

Both genetic components are modelled, not just the additive one. Heterosis is where hybrid advantage comes from, and dropping it costs real accuracy.

02 — ranking

Ranked against the field

Hybrid yields sit in a narrow band, so a lone figure tells you little. Every cross is placed against every alternative you could make instead.

03 — honesty

Limits stated up front

Accuracy depends on how related a cross is to what the model has seen. That figure is shown with every prediction, and the ranking is withheld when it would not hold up.

What this tool does not do. It predicts yield; it does not judge whether a cross can be made. Feasibility depends on anther extrusion, flowering synchrony and plant height difference — traits fixed when the trial was designed. The training data contains only successful crosses, so there are no failures to learn from.

measured performance

Methodology

How well does it predict?

Predictive ability is the Pearson correlation between predicted and observed yield — the standard metric in genomic prediction. It depends far more on how related a cross is to the training set than on the model itself.

Reproduction of the published protocol

The implementation was rebuilt in Python and checked against Zhao et al. (2021) and its supplementary tables.

Why ranking rather than the raw number

Observed hybrids average 10.08 Mg/ha with a standard deviation of 0.35, and BLUP shrinks predictions toward that mean. Absolute values cluster tightly while the ordering stays informative — and ordering is what a Pearson correlation measures, and what a breeder with limited plots actually needs.

Not modelled

Scope. Calibrated on Central European elite winter wheat (Experimental Series II: 1,725 hybrids from 178 female × 39 male parents). Performance on germplasm outside this pool is uncharacterised — a cross between two parents the model has never seen falls to r ≈ 0.26.
